The Yield Gap in Modern Agriculture

Jun 1, 2026 · 9 min read · First Mover Fund

The world is not running out of land. It is running out of productive land, and that distinction is worth billions.

What is the Yield Gap?

Agricultural scientists define the yield gap as the difference between what a piece of land actually produces and what it is theoretically capable of producing under optimal management conditions. In practical terms, actual farm yields across the world represent only 50 to 80 percent of what that same land could sustainably produce.

The numbers are most striking in developing regions. In sub-Saharan Africa, many crops yield less than 20 percent of their theoretical capacity — meaning that more than four-fifths of the land's productive potential goes unrealised. This is not a natural limitation of the soil. It is a management gap, and management gaps can be closed.

Why Farmland Productivity Matters Now

The UN projects that global food demand will rise by 70 percent by 2050. The population feeding that demand cannot simply clear new land — environmental constraints, regulatory frameworks, and the slow degradation of frontier ecosystems mean that the solution must come from improving existing land, not expanding into new territory.

At the same time, the regenerative agriculture sector is expanding at approximately 16 percent annually, and $1 trillion in investable farmland exists globally. U.S. farmland has averaged approximately 10 percent annual returns over the past several decades, combining income from crop yields with long-term land appreciation. The opportunity is well-established. The question is where the next generation of returns will come from.

What Causes the Yield Gap?

Four factors account for the majority of the global yield gap. Soil degradation tops the list: researchers estimate that 90 percent of Earth's land surface could face degradation by 2050 under business-as-usual agricultural practices. Degraded soil holds less water, supports fewer microorganisms, and releases rather than sequesters carbon.

Outdated techniques persist because change is expensive and risky for individual farmers operating on thin margins. Poor water management compounds the problem — inconsistent irrigation either under-waters or over-waters crops, both reducing yields and accelerating soil erosion. Finally, weak market access means that even when yields improve, farmers in remote regions cannot consistently convert that improvement into income.

"The yield gap is not a natural feature of the land. It is a management gap — and management gaps can be closed by the right team with the right capital."

How Regenerative Farming Closes the Gap

Regenerative agriculture encompasses a set of practices that work with natural systems rather than against them. No-till farming preserves soil structure and moisture retention. Cover cropping adds organic matter and prevents erosion between growing seasons. Precision agriculture uses sensor data and mapping to apply water and nutrients exactly where they are needed. Integrating livestock into crop rotations accelerates soil regeneration through natural fertilisation cycles.

The financial results are documented. A 2018 study found that corn produced via regenerative methods generated profits 78 percent higher than conventional approaches — primarily because improved soil health reduced input costs and increased yields simultaneously. The profitability case for regenerative transition is no longer theoretical.

The Investment Opportunity

The investment thesis follows from the agronomic facts. Identify underperforming land. Apply superior management at scale. Capture value through increased revenues from improved crop yields and enhanced land restoration value — plus, where applicable, certified CO2 capture sold to corporate buyers.

Investors who position themselves in undervalued, underperforming land before institutional capital arrives capture the majority of the rerating that follows as productivity improvements are validated and certified. This is the first-mover advantage in its most literal sense: the improvement is real, the demand is structural, and the timing window is finite.

First Mover Fund's Approach

The result is a dual revenue stream: income from improved crop yields, and income from certified CO2 capture sold to corporations meeting their emission reduction commitments.
